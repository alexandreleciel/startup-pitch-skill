# Deck library: curated study set

The library is an index of real, money-raising startup pitch decks (440 rows in `assets/library/pitch-decks.csv`, 2003 to 2026, each row pointing to a public aggregator page where the deck is embedded or shown as slide images). This file is the hand-curated subset: 25 decks whose slide content was checked for this skill, grouped by the pattern they teach, with the slide order and the lessons that generalise. Use the CSV for breadth (search with `python3 scripts/library.py`), this file for depth.

## How to use the library during pitch work

1. Find three comparables: `python3 scripts/library.py --sector <sector> --round <round> --year-from <year> --limit 10`, prefer rows with `verified_status ok` and a complete round, year and amount. If the user names a company, `--company <name>`.
2. Study structure and headline patterns, not numbers: how many slides, what comes before the product, where traction sits, how the ask is framed. Compare with the curated set below for the same pattern.
3. Cite decks by name and year in the user's deck notes or script, for example "see Front Series A deck (2016)" or "compare the Airbnb 2008 market-validation slide". Never copy a comparable's numbers, projections or claims into the user's deck; their numbers come from their own business.
4. If a deck is absent, say so and point to `assets/library/unavailable.csv` (Higgsfield, Stripe, OpenAI, SpaceX and the others listed there have no public fundraising deck in the indexed sources as of 2026-11-10).

Verification legend: "viewed" means the slide images were downloaded from the source URL and read for this file on 2026-11-10. "Structure from secondary description" means the content was not personally viewed and the outline comes from the aggregator's page text. Amounts are as reported by the aggregators (round amounts in the CSV are in USD; EUR and GBP amounts converted at fixed rates noted in the CSV).

## Pattern 1: traction-led seed decks

### Buffer, seed, 2011, $500K, 13 slides (viewed)
Source: https://www.alexanderjarvis.com/buffer-startup-pitch-deck/
Structure: 1 logo; 2 "Social, the most important trend" (Zuckerberg's Law quote); 3 "How do you use social to drive traffic?"; 4 "Queue your updates" (product screenshot); 5 Traction (800 paying users, $150K annual run rate, 97% margins, 55,000 users growing 40% per month, 1.5M updates buffered); 6 Milestones (timeline from launch Jan 2011 to projected 1M users); 7 Business Model (freemium, 2% free-to-paid, 5% churn, LTV $240, so up to $5 to acquire a free user); 8 Social Media Landscape; 9 "The effect of Buffering" (ReadWriteWeb quote); 10 "A sharing standard" (integrations); 11 Competitive Landscape; 12 Team, advisors, previous investors; 13 contact email.
Lessons: traction appears by slide 5 and every number has a unit and a growth rate; the business model slide is a unit-economics argument (conversion, churn, LTV, CAC ceiling) rather than a pricing table; one third-party quote stands in for a press slide; the deck is 13 slides with one idea per slide; the team slide also lists advisors and prior investors as social proof.

### Coinbase, seed, 2012, $600K, 11 slides plus closing (viewed)
Source: https://www.alexanderjarvis.com/coinbase-pitch-deck-raise-seed-capital-investment/
Structure: 1 "Your hosted bitcoin wallet" with product shot; 2 "Bitcoin: A New Digital Currency" (instant, international, no transaction fees); 3 same title, world map; 4 Bitcoin Growth ($2M per day transaction volume chart); 5 Early Adopters (use-case word cloud); 6 "Too Difficult To Use" (command-line tools vs Coinbase); 7 "Coinbase: A Hosted Bitcoin Wallet"; 8 "Coinbase : Bitcoin :: iTunes : MP3"; 9 Coinbase Signups (20% daily growth); 10 Coinbase Transactions ($65,000 USD in the first 5 weeks); 11 contact.
Lessons: the deck sells the underlying wave first (four slides on Bitcoin) and the product second; one analogy slide carries the positioning; two traction charts with a single annotated number each close the deck; no team slide, no financials, no ask slide in the public version, which only works when traction is steep.

### TransferWise (now Wise), seed, May 2011, GBP 650K ask (aggregators list $1.3M, 2012), 9 slides (viewed)
Source: https://www.alexanderjarvis.com/transferwise-pitch-deck-raise-seed-round-capital-investment/
Structure: 1 cover "Peer-to-peer currency exchange", founders and date; 2 Currency Exchange before and after (banks take 3 to 6% hidden margin; mid-market rate plus GBP 1 flat fee); 3 How does it work? (4 steps plus screenshot); 4 Traction (charging customers from day one, GBP 1M+ sent, 70% volume from repeat customers, GBP 0 marketing, ~20% monthly organic growth, two charts); 5 Roadmap (segments, currencies, marketing); 6 Competition (retail FX brokers, banks, CurrencyFair, CurrencyCloud, PayPal); 7 Team; 8 Financing (raising GBP 650K to last until end of 2012, four goals); 9 Questions?
Lessons: the problem is a single before/after diagram with the fee made visible; "charging from day one" and "GBP 0 spent on marketing" are the headline traction claims; the financing slide pairs the amount with the runway and the milestones it buys; nine slides are enough for a seed round when the numbers are real.

### Intercom, pre-seed convertible, 2011, $600K (aggregators), 7 slides marked DRAFT (viewed)
Source: https://www.alexanderjarvis.com/intercom-pitch-deck-pre-seed-convertible-fundraise/
Structure: 1 logo; 2 The team (four founders, Contrast consultancy, Qwitter and Exceptional both acquired); 3 The problem (building relationships with customers is impossible for SaaS providers; no single tool for discovery, research, communication and relationship management); 4 The solution (install like Google Analytics, browse customers, research individuals, in-app messaging, relationship metric); 5 The market (SaaS providers $21B, $93B in 2016 per Forrester; mobile and desktop later); 6 Landscape and competitors (table by category); 7 Progress (product in testing since January, ready for public beta, beta testers love it, Jason Fried tweet).
Lessons: team first when the founders' track record is the strongest asset; the problem slide reads as a thesis, in sentences, not bullets of pain points; the competitor slide is a category map that shows the gap rather than a 2x2; "progress" replaces "traction" pre-launch and is backed by one named endorsement.

### Sorare, seed, 2020, ~$4M (e.ventures lead), 22 slides (viewed)
Source: https://www.alexanderjarvis.com/sorare-pitch-deck-to-raise-4m-seed-round/
Structure: 1 Global Fantasy Soccer; 2 Founding Team; 3 "We can not collect digital goods"; 4 "A new market is taking off: blockchain gaming" (ERC721 2018, leagues adoption 2019, early traction EUR 1M sales); 5 "People love to collect goods" ($370B collectibles per year); 6 Game Weekly Loop; 7 Huge Market Opportunity ($20B fantasy sports, $25B licensing, $250B betting); 8 "We design a mainstream game" (no crypto knowledge, simple payment, official licenses); 9 Competitive advantage (licenses, community, technology); 10 Growth: monthly sales chart; 11 Licenses: 70 clubs represented; 12 Customer acquisition 100% organic; 13 "Collecting requires scarcity"; 14 Pre-Seed round April 2019 EUR 545K (investor logos); 15 What we achieved in 10 months (team of 6, 76 clubs, EUR 1M sales); 16 community side games; 17 Vision; 18 Round status: full, closing May 12th; 19 Appendix; 20 opportunity for clubs; 21 why blockchain; 22 community quotes.
Lessons: a crypto product pitched as a mainstream game (slide 8 lists what the user does not need to know); the "what we achieved in 10 months" slide pairs three numbers with the time it took; prior investors and the round status are shown as slides, signalling momentum; the appendix carries the technical "why blockchain" so the main story stays about football fans.

## Pattern 2: insight-led decks (the thesis before the product)

### Dropbox, seed, 2007 (Sequoia; aggregators list the amount as N/A or $1.2M), 16 slides (viewed)
Source: https://www.alexanderjarvis.com/dropbox-pitch-deck-to-raise-seed-capital-investment/
Structure: 1 "Dropbox: Moving the world's files"; 2 "Storage is a mess" (photo); 3 "It's 2007, and it's still a pain to..."; 4 "What are people doing now?"; 5 "In a perfect world..."; 6 Dropbox (keeps files in sync, backed up, accessible, easy to share; it just works); 7 Demo; 8 Why now? (devices, remote teams, falling bandwidth and storage prices, online storage unclaimed "much like search pre-Google"); 9 Why better?; 10 "What did they screw up?" (competitors); 11 Technical advantages; 12 Team (two founders, photo of moving into the first office); 13 Business model (freemium, SMB per seat, platform); 14 Platform; 15 Customer acquisition; 16 "Ranked #1 among summer YC startups" (VentureBeat quote).
Lessons: three slides of problem framing written as questions before the product appears; a "why now" slide with four structural shifts; competitors are discussed as "what did they screw up", which doubles as the product requirements list; the demo sits in the middle, not the end; the deck closes on external validation rather than an ask.

### LinkedIn, Series B, August 2004, $10M (Greylock), 37 slides plus appendix (viewed, with Reid Hoffman's annotations)
Source: https://www.reidhoffman.org/linkedin-pitch-to-greylock/
Structure: 1 cover; 2 "Find and Contact the People You Need Through the People You Already Trust"; 3 LinkedIn is Professional People Search 2.0 (three revenue components); 4 Professional People Search 1.0 uses flat directories (the problem); 5 2.0 leverages networks; 6 to 9 analogies (eBay, PayPal, Google each as "2.0" of their category, then LinkedIn); 10 Establishing the network: our first priority (PayPal waited until 4M users before turning on revenue); 11 Network growth: commitment from Series A; 12 Network growth: success (actual above projection); 13 to 15 LinkedIn leads this market (pie charts and competitor map); 16 Growth includes major groups; 17 Strong results with less than $4M spent; 18 Great inbound business development; 19 Market-leading position creates strong barriers to entry; 20 to 22 The network enables revenue (eBay, PayPal, Google analogies, then LinkedIn); 23 Market opportunity is large (comparables); 24 to 30 the three revenue lines, each with its market (InLeads vs AdWords, Opportunities vs online jobs, Network Plus vs online personals); 31 Revenue is near-term (pricing and launch timing); 32 Internet 2.0 business model: high revenue and high margins; 33 to 34 Series B brings LinkedIn to operating profitability in 2005 (assumptions, five-year financials); 35 Business and tech team; 36 Board and other investors; 37 LinkedIn is well-positioned in an essential market; closing; appendix.
Lessons: the deck answers the investor's prior objection first (no revenue yet) with the PayPal precedent and a "network first, revenue second" thesis; every slide title is a complete sentence that states the claim; the Series A promise is shown next to the actual curve ("we did what we said"); analogies are used in sets of three, then applied to LinkedIn; the ask is framed as the path to profitability, not as a budget. Hoffman's own commentary on the page lists what he would change (fewer slides, clearer revenue timing).

### Mixpanel, Series B, 2014, $65M (a16z), 13 slides (12 visible on the source page, viewed)
Source: https://www.alexanderjarvis.com/mixpanel-series-b-startup-pitch-deck/
Structure: 1 logo; 2 Problem 1 ("Most of the world will make decisions by either guessing or using their gut"); 3 Problem 2 (companies measure vanity metrics); 4 Solution (analytics for product and marketing, sales and finance next); 5 Mission "Help the world learn from its data"; 6 Competitive advantage (the 2010 database engine); 7 Monthly recurring revenue over time (values redacted, growth rates shown: 405% Sept 2011 to 2012); 8 Sales KPIs; 9 Marketing (August spend snapshot); 10 2015/2016 expansion plan; 11 Competition 2x2 (free vs paid, startups vs incumbents); 12 Financing history (2009 $15K YC, 2009 $500K, 2011 $1.25M Sequoia, 2012 $10.25M Series A a16z).
Lessons: one sentence per slide in the first half; two problem slides, one about the customer and one about the industry; a growth-stage deck still shows the raw MRR chart, with growth rates as the headline; the operating plan (sales headcount, churn targets, new offices) is a slide, not an appendix; the financing history closes the deck and shows who already backed the company.

### Tinder (MatchBox), seed, 2012, 10 slides (viewed)
Source: https://www.alexanderjarvis.com/tinder-pitch-deck-seed-stage-startup/
Structure: 1 "MatchBox, the flirting game"; 2 "Meet Matt" (photo); 3 Matt spots a girl he likes at a party, but won't go over to say hello; 4 He has the same problem most of us do: fear of rejection; 5 "Meet the cure" (app, sign in with Facebook); 6 Like or Not screen; 7 Matches screen; 8 Messages screen; 9 Under the hood (hyper-location via wifi, mutual liking, social interests, friends in common); 10 Revenue opportunity (two matches free then $1 each, virtual gifts, pay to rank higher).
Lessons: a single persona carries the whole problem statement; the product is shown as three consecutive screens instead of a feature list; the insight ("mutual liking removes rejection") is stated once and then demonstrated; monetisation is one slide of concrete mechanics; no market-size, team or competition slide, which an early consumer deck can get away with only when the demo is self-explanatory.

### Wise (US embedded business banking, not TransferWise), Series A, October 2020, $12M, 21 slides (viewed)
Source: https://www.alexanderjarvis.com/wise-pitch-deck-to-raise-12m-series-a-round/
Structure: 1 Embedded Business Banking; 2 agenda (thesis, differentiation, acquisition via partners, business model, traction, timeline, Series A); 3 to 5 "We believe small businesses..." (go digital, thrive in ecosystems, want speed of pay); 6 Where things break down (banking, bring-your-own-bank, slow money); 7 Wise fixes it: digital, embedded, instant; 8 thesis statement; 9 What differentiates Wise?; 10 Wise acquires via partners (B2B2B, $0 CAC); 11 "Launch banking in 2 weeks"; 12 partner reasons; 13 small-business reasons; 14 partner onboarding in three weeks; 15 Business model (deposits ~100 bps, interchange ~100 bps, ~$600 ARR per business); 16 50% revenue share with partners; 17 10K+ business applications, $10M+ moved; 18 Partner traction (2 live, 3 in pilot, 31+ in pipeline); 19 Built with best in class (BBVA, Visa, Plaid, Stripe, AWS); 20 team; 21 "Our story so far" timeline from pre-seed Q1 2019 to Series A.
Lessons: a thesis stated as three beliefs before any product; the distribution model (partners) gets as many slides as the product; business model and partner economics are two separate slides with one number each; the timeline slide at the end shows speed of execution. Listed here because aggregators file it under "Wise", which is easy to confuse with TransferWise.

## Pattern 3: marketplace liquidity decks

### Airbnb (AirBed & Breakfast), seed, 2008 ($600K, Sequoia, closed April 2009), 14 slides (viewed)
Source: https://www.alexanderjarvis.com/airbnb-seed-pitch-deck/ (same 14 slides as the Business Insider 2011 article and SlideShare 65585661, which pitchdeckhunt.com and bestpitchdeck.com embed)
Structure: 1 Welcome, tagline "Book rooms with locals, rather than hotels"; 2 Problem (price matters when booking travel; hotels disconnect you from the city; no easy way to book with a local or become a host); 3 Solution (a web platform to save money when traveling, make money when hosting, share culture); 4 Market Validation (Couchsurfing 660,000 users; Craigslist 50,000 temporary housing listings per week); 5 Market Size (2B+ trips booked, 560M budget and online, 84M trips with AB&B); 6 Product (search by city, review listings, book it); 7 Business Model (10% commission; 84M trips x $25 average fee = $2.1B revenue projected by 2011); 8 Adoption Strategy (events, partnerships, Craigslist dual posting); 9 Competition (2x2: affordable vs expensive, offline vs online transaction); 10 Competitive Advantages (first to market, host incentive, list once, ease of use, profiles, design and brand); 11 Team (Gebbia, Chesky, Blecharczyk, advisor Michael Seibel); 12 Press; 13 User Testimonials; 14 Financial ($500K angel round; 80K trips, $2M revenue over 12 months).
Lessons: market validation (proof that people already do this badly elsewhere) comes before market size; the business model is one multiplication on one slide; the adoption strategy names specific events and a specific growth hack (Craigslist); competition is drawn on axes that put the company alone in the best quadrant; the ask ties the money to a transaction target and a revenue number over 12 months. The structure (problem, solution, validation, size, product, model, adoption, competition, advantages, team, press, testimonials, ask) is the template most later seed decks copy.

### Uber (UberCab), seed, 2008, $200K (aggregators), 25 slides (viewed)
Source: https://www.alexanderjarvis.com/uber-pitch-deck-raise-seed-capital-investment/
Structure: 1 UberCab, "Next-Generation Car Service"; 2 Cabs in 2008; 3 The Medallion System; 4 UberCab Concept ("the NetJets of car services"); 5 1-Click Car Service; 6 Key Differentiators; 7 Operating Principles; 8 UberCab apps; 9 UberCab.com; 10 Use-Cases; 11 User Benefits; 12 Environmental Benefits; 13 UberCab Fleet; 14 Initial Service Area (central SF, then Manhattan); 15 Technology; 16 Demand Forecasting; 17 Overall Market ($4.2B, top 4 players 22% of revenue); 18 Composition of Market; 19 Target Cities; 20 Potential Outcomes (best case $1B+ yearly revenue; realistic 5% of top 5 US cities, $20 to 30M profit; worst case a 100-client service in SF); 21 SmartPhones Aug 2008; 22 Future Optimizations; 23 Marketing Ideas; 24 Location-Based Service; 25 Progress to Date (next: buy 3 cars, develop app, Feb 1 demo, "raise a few million").
Lessons: text-heavy bullets and 25 slides, so the famous deck is not a design model; what works is the scenario slide (best, realistic, worst outcomes stated in revenue terms), the "why now" built from smartphone adoption data, and a closing progress slide that lists concrete next steps; the original pitch was a premium, members-only service, a reminder that the first deck describes the wedge, not the eventual company.

### Divvy Homes, Series A, April 2018, $30M (a16z), 14 slides (viewed; numbers redacted as $XK and Y%)
Source: https://www.alexanderjarvis.com/divvy-pitch-deck-to-raise-30m-series-a/ (pitchdeckhunt.com lists the same company under divvy-homes, 2019)
Structure: 1 "Access to home ownership. For everyone."; 2 Team (real estate, capital markets, technology); 3 "6 million credit-worthy households should be homeowners, but aren't"; 4 Solution: fractional homeownership (tenant picks the home, Divvy buys it, tenant buys out the equity); 5 Technology is core (pricing, underwriting, operations); 6 Unit economics per home; 7 Market expansion (stable, high-yield metros on a map); 8 Credit facility (validate, growth, scale, off balance sheet); 9 Product demo; 10 Value proposition for both parties; 11 Distribution: agents are incentivised (referral loop); 12 Traction: monthly homes closed; 13 The future: a new asset class; 14 Mission: 100,000 families.
Lessons: a two-sided real-estate marketplace pitched with the capital side (credit facility) as its own slide; one sentence with one number states the problem; distribution is a loop diagram, not a channel list; the traction chart shows the supply-side metric (homes closed) rather than sign-ups; each slide carries a one-line takeaway bar at the bottom.

## Pattern 4: consumer growth decks

### YouTube, seed, 2005, $3.5M (Sequoia), 10 slides (viewed; the source page appends four charts that were not in the original)
Source: https://www.alexanderjarvis.com/youtube-seed-stage-startup-pitch-deck/
Structure: 1 logo, "Broadcast Yourself."; 2 Company Purpose (primary outlet of user-generated video; upload, share, browse); 3 Problem (video files too large to email or host, no standard formats, videos exist as isolated files); 4 Solution (upload, encode to Flash, community); 5 Market Size (cheap digital video recording, broadband at critical mass); 6 Competition (OurMedia, Open Media Network, Google Video; PutFile, DailyMotion, Vimeo); 7 Product Development; 8 Sales and Distribution (advertising, paid distribution, premium features, premium content); 9 Team (Chen, Hurley, Karim, all PayPal); 10 Metrics ("Launched June 11th. Has already overtaken all previously existing competitors").
Lessons: this is the Sequoia template (purpose, problem, solution, why now, market, competition, product, business model, team) executed in ten plain text slides; "market size" is argued as two enabling shifts, not a dollar figure; the metrics slide is a single sentence of relative position; the team slide leans entirely on the PayPal pedigree.

### BuzzFeed, Series A, 2008, $3.5M, about 10 slides (viewed; the source page also mixes in unrelated Rocket Internet slides, ignore slides 2 to 12 there)
Source: https://www.alexanderjarvis.com/buzzfeed-startup-pitch-deck/
Structure: 1 cover; Market Direction: Ads as Content (Google and YouTube screenshots annotated organic vs sponsored); Advertising as Content on BuzzFeed; Distributed Promotion (widgets); Custom Promotion (branded microsites); Trend Targeting; Competitors: Media / Ads (Venn diagram with BuzzFeed in the overlap); How Big Can This Get? (global technology platform for trend-centric advertising; "YouTube/Wikipedia for buzz"); The Team (Peretti, Lerer, Johnson, Duncan Watts, Jason Kottke); Contact.
Lessons: a media deck that starts with the shift in advertising, not with the content; each product slide is an annotated screenshot of an existing page; competition is drawn as two circles with the company in the intersection; the vision slide uses two analogies in one line.

### Monzo, Series B era investment overview, 2016 to 2017 (GBP 19.3M per aggregators), 15 slides (viewed)
Source: https://www.alexanderjarvis.com/monzo-pitch-deck-raise-series-b-capital-investment/
Structure: 1 Investment Overview; 2 "a financial control centre to 1 billion people worldwide"; 3 "Today, Monzo is a bank that's as smart as your phone" (UK banking licence, in-house tech); 4 More than 120,000 people have a funded Monzo account (organic growth chart; GBP 1M crowdfunded in 96 seconds); 5 Customer metrics (120,000 cards, 4.3 App Store stars, 60% active, GBP 370 average monthly spend, 324 transactions per year vs UK average 115); 6 the marketplace bank (partner logos); 7 team (product, tech and banking); 8 viral growth mechanics to the first million customers; 9 Monzo Community (Twitter followers, forum posts, crowdfunding, name suggestions); 10 machine learning for fraud, credit scoring and support; 11 95% of bank software built in-house; 12 UK banking licence received August 2016 (regulatory timeline); 13 How will Monzo make money? (lending now, profit sharing and affiliate later); 14 additional capital for Europe, US and Asia; 15 closing: product, technology and regulation.
Lessons: consumer growth is shown as community evidence (crowdfunding speed, forum activity) as well as a user curve; engagement is benchmarked against the national average; the regulatory milestone is a timeline slide, which is the pattern for any licensed business; the revenue model is honest about being early ("in the short and medium term, lending").

## Pattern 5: deep tech and science-led decks

### Theranos, pre-IPO style round, June 2006, $30M offering (aggregators label it Series C), 23 slides (viewed)
Source: https://www.alexanderjarvis.com/theranos-pitch-deck-raise-series-c-capital-investment/
Structure: 1 "A Presentation For Investors"; 2 Theranos, Inc. (goal, initial market Phase IV clinical trials, product platform, founded 2003, 50 employees); 3 Theranos Today (6 deals, 5 companies, $120 to 300M revenue in the next 1.5 years, existing deals $120M to $1.5Bn); 4 Management and board; 5 What is our system?; 6 Technology and products (cartridge, reader, informatics); 7 Theranos ABCS system diagram; 8 Product Features (5 to 10 uL blood, results in under 30 minutes); 9 Value Proposition; 10 to 13 clinical scenario charts (chemotherapy monitoring, dose customization, real-time PK/PD); 14 Total Available Market: Phase IV clinical trials ($39B per year); 15 TAM: pharma preclinical ($10B); 16 Value proposition revenue (information fee, 70% margins); 17 Pains in the existing marketplace; 18 Theranos Solution; 19 Drivers for Success (first mover, patents); 20 Offering ($30M; existing investors $15 to 20M, new $10 to 15M); 21 Existing Investors; 22 Technology vs today's lab; 23 Computation of results.
Lessons (as a cautionary example): the deck leads with revenue claims from unnamed pharma deals and places the technology evidence in the last two slides; the clinical charts are illustrative scenarios, not trial data; the offering slide shows insiders taking most of the round; the TAM is the whole clinical-trials budget. For a science-led deck the order should be the reverse: evidence, then validation partners, then market. Theranos was later exposed as fraudulent and its founder convicted (2022), which is why this deck is in the library.

## Pattern 6: fintech and regulated-business decks

### Brex, Series B, 2018, $57M, 19 slides (viewed; unit economics table redacted)
Source: https://www.alexanderjarvis.com/brex-pitch-deck-to-raise-57m-series-b/
Structure: 1 cover; 2 Team and Investors (Pagar.me founders; Ribbit, YC, Thiel, Levchin, Pascarella, Elkann, Telles, Sicupira, Lemann); 3 Entrepreneurs Can't Get Credit Cards (two rejection letters as the slide); 4 Corporate Cards Have No Controls; 5 Banks Use Outdated Technology; 6 Starting with a Corporate Credit Card (concentric market: global B2B payments $101B, Fortune 500 $25.6B, US venture-backed companies $5.2B); 7 Initial Focus on Technology Companies (underwriting arbitrage, big spenders, early adopters, high growth, concentrated); 8 Compelling Value Proposition at Acquisition (higher limits, no personal guarantee, instant signup); 9 Easy to Switch To, Hard to Switch From; 10 to 13 four product pillars (better data, better controls, receipt capture, ERP integrations), each with a named customer logo; 14 Enhancing Retention via Rewards (AWS spend = $500K AMEX points); 15 Growing Volume in Referral-Only Private Beta (monthly bars); 16 Customers Exhibit "Negative Churn" (spend funnel by customer stage); 17 Highly Attractive Unit Economics (table by stage); 18 Brex Investment Value Proposition; 19 closing.
Lessons: the problem is shown as evidence (real rejection letters) before it is explained; the market slide narrows from the whole category to the wedge segment and states why that segment is underwritable; each product slide is anchored by a customer logo; growth is shown for a referral-only beta, so the slope rather than the absolute number is the claim; the investor list on slide 2 does a lot of the credibility work in a regulated category.

### Alan, Series A, November 2017, EUR 23M (about $28M, Index), 42 slides (viewed; metrics redacted as EUR X and X%)
Source: https://www.alexanderjarvis.com/alan-pitch-deck-to-raise-28m-series-a/
Structure: 1 cover "Investor Deck, November 2017"; 2 "We are Alan. Health insurance made simple."; 3 first digital health insurer in Europe (first independent insurer licensed in France since 1986); 4 Why we are raising Series A (leader in France, expand in Europe, healthcare platform); 5 to 14 product section (company sign-up in 5 minutes, employee sign-up, admin tools, the plan, the card, reimbursements, health events in one place, customer service, user tweets); 15 to 19 market section (EUR 36bn French private health insurance, 96% of people covered, 500+ players; EUR 11bn prevoyance upsell; Europe; competitive landscape); 20 to 27 key metrics and growth (unit metrics, cumulative contracts, ARR, distribution 100% online direct, acquisition channels, seasonality, ARR objectives); 28 to 30 "It's all about execution" (licence in under 6 months, lean team, CAC near zero in the first 9 months, capital efficiency); 31 to 36 team (photo, founders, three team slides, organisation tools); 37 "2016: one of the largest seed rounds in France" (EUR 12M, CNP, Partech, Power Corp, angels); 38 to 41 next steps (product roadmap 2017, better underwriting through data, team evolution to 2020); 42 closing.
Lessons: a regulated insurer states the licence as the first proof point and repeats it as an execution win; the ask is explained on slide 4 before any product; the deck is long because it is sectioned (product, market, metrics, execution, team, next steps) with divider slides, so the reader can skip; seasonality of the market is explained rather than hidden; the previous round is a slide of its own.

### Carta, Series D, late 2018, $150M, 23 slides (viewed)
Source: https://www.alexanderjarvis.com/carta-pitch-deck-to-raise-150m-series-d/
Structure: 1 Carta investor pitch; 2 The problem (a private share certificate costs $20K, a public share $7); 3 The solution: build the central registry of asset ownership; 4 How? (issuer to owner, online); 5 to 8 what the product does (issue securities, automate approval and compliance, track the cap table, track the portfolio); 9 Venture capital is our first network; 10 Our second network is Limited Partners; 11 Sell software and financial products into each node in the network; 12 "And keep going..." (asset categories by size); 13 "So how are we doing?"; 14 Carta at a glance (founded 2012, 7 offices, 450 employees, $50M ARR, ~700,000 shareholders, ~10,000 subscription customers); 15 Annual recurring revenue (quarterly bars); 16 Subscription customers; 17 Payback period (CAC vs new ARR); 18 Over 700,000 shareholders; 19 "Lastly..."; 20 Ownership as simple as payroll (Uber drivers, Cargill employees, Airbnb hosts, marked "not current customers, this is our vision"); 21 This is a global problem; 22 "Think of us as a utility company. We lay financial cables" (Bell System analogy); 23 Thank You.
Lessons: a late-stage deck still spends four slides on the problem and the mechanism; the network-expansion story (companies, then VCs, then LPs) explains why each new product is cheaper to sell; traction is three charts plus a one-slide summary with six numbers; the vision slide labels aspirational examples honestly; the closing analogy (utility company) tells the investor how to value the business.

## Pattern 7: Series A metrics decks (SaaS)

### Front, Series A, 2016, $10M, 21 slides (viewed)
Source: https://www.alexanderjarvis.com/front-series-saas-startup-pitch-deck/ (also pitchdeckhunt.com/pitch-decks/front and SlideShare front-series-a-deck-64596550, uploaded by Mathilde Collin)
Structure: 1 cover "All your company's external communications in one collaborative inbox"; 2 The Problem (215B emails per day, 54% business, 7% YoY; email was designed for personal use); 3 The Solution (collaborative, integrated, unified); 4 Competitive Landscape (2x2); 5 We Have a Head Start (shared inboxes as the wedge); 6 Select Customers; 7 Customers Are Very Satisfied (quotes with seat counts); 8 Consistent Organic Growth (MRR grew 5.4x in 12 months); 9 Low Churn (~3% user churn, negative net MRR churn); 10 divider "Our Focus to Date"; 11 The Product Comes First (60% of payroll is engineering, zero support agents); 12 Acquisition Channels (organic 70% of leads, sales, marketing, with per-channel numbers); 13 We Land and Expand (cohort table, 150% annual expansion); 14 We've Been Capital Efficient ($1.3M spent to reach $1.4M ARR, $1.8M left, $90K monthly burn, 5 months to profitability); 15 "Raising $10M Series A to Accelerate Growth"; 16 The Right Mix of Passion and Experience (team); 17 The Product Roadmap Is Clear; 18 Projections (ARR $3M 2016 to $10M 2017, headcount 39 to 53); 19 Funding (seed $3.1M Sept 2014, Series A $10M with named committed angels); 20 The Opportunity (Slack proved businesses buy communication tools; Front will own external communications); 21 Thanks.
Lessons: every slide title is the claim and the body is the evidence; the metrics block (growth, churn, channels, cohorts, capital efficiency) sits in the middle and is five slides long; the ask slide is a divider, and what follows it (team, roadmap, projections, funding) is "what the money does"; the capital-efficiency slide is the strongest single slide in the deck because it gives four numbers an investor can check against each other; the cohort table is shown raw.

### Mattermark, first Series A deck, late 2013 to early 2014, $6.5M (Foundry), 33 slides (viewed; the source page appends a second deck from September 2014)
Source: https://www.alexanderjarvis.com/mattermark-pitch-deck-series/
Structure: 1 "organizing the world's business information"; 2 Founders (Twilio, Microsoft, YC, 500 Startups); 3 "the greatest minds of my generation are making spreadsheets"; 4 knowledge workers are a human API; 5 asking and re-asking the same questions; 6 expensive questions, queries and reports (concentric market from $100B+ global business processes to $60M venture capital); 7 "There has to be a more intelligent way"; 8 1st market: private investors; 9 Quarterly Revenue; 10 Accounts; 11 Monthly Active Users; 12 Mattermark Pro usage; 13 finance is just the beginning (Morningstar, S&P, Bloomberg revenues); 14 paid customer development, already more than a VC tool; 15 join our team; 16 Appendix divider; 17 Weekly Active Users; 18 Monthly Paid Churn Rate; 19 Go To Market divider; 20 Email subscribers = leads; 21 Newsletter readership; 22 Website acquisition funnel; 23 Signup page conversion; 24 Trial to paid conversion; 25 Lessons learned from 6 months of SaaS sales; 26 Hiring plan divider; 27 to 30 org charts at current, 6, 12 and 18 months with ARR targets ($550K to $5M); 31 Simple budget (why targeting $5 to 7M); 32 Compounding is powerful; 33 closing.
Lessons: a 15-slide main deck with an 18-slide appendix is a workable format, the appendix holding every funnel chart an analyst will ask for; the first market (VCs) is framed as the wedge into a much larger data business, using incumbents' revenues as the comparable; the hiring plan is shown as org charts tied to ARR milestones, which converts the ask into a staffing plan; "lessons learned from 6 months of sales" is an unusual slide that signals operating honesty.

### Moz (SEOmoz), Series B, July 2011 (sought $20 to 25M; Foundry led $18M in 2012), 36 slides (viewed)
Source: https://www.alexanderjarvis.com/moz-pitch-deck-series-b-startup/ (Rand Fishkin also published it on SlideShare as randfish/seomoz-pitch-deck-july-2011)
Structure: 1 The Next Stage of Moz; 2 A Little Moz History (timeline 1981 to 2011); 3 How'd we do that?; 4 Inbound Marketing (all the free traffic sources); 5 what we want to help other companies measure; 6 Macroeconomic trends that benefit Moz; 7 Marketing spend still unbalanced vs behaviour; 8 It's a data-driven world; 9 Problem(s) we're here to solve; 10 Organic web marketing is poorly understood; 11 The web marketer's weekly analytics challenge; 12 Specific, painful web marketing tasks; 13 Our target market; 14 marketer Venn diagram; 15 marketers who focus on organic; 16 Immediate customer targets; 17 Where are we today?; 18 metrics ($12 to 13M estimated 2011 revenue, $10.8M run rate, 13,500 PRO subscribers, ~100 new trials per day, LTV ~$900, CAC ~$100); 19 more metrics (57% trial conversion, 25% churn in first two paid months, 82% gross margin, ~$1M net profit); 20 Planned investment round; 21 raising $20 to 25M, $6 to 7M founder liquidity, $13 to 19M to the balance sheet, new board; 22 Business risks; 23 to 26 four named risks (Google integrates SEO analytics; the web becomes less open; we fail to adapt; reputation missteps); 27 Use of funds 2011 to 2012; 28 wider audience with expanded product; 29 two-year team roadmap 40 to 100 people; 30 investments by function; 31 Potential acquisitions; 32 Why Moz is uniquely positioned; 33 four reasons (community of 300K+ marketers, technology lead, 2x+ growth for four years, culture); 34 the ask ("Seattle's next $1 billion+ company"); 35 to 36 repeat of reasons and ask.
Lessons: a profitable company still shows LTV, CAC, churn and trial conversion on two dense slides; the round is specified including founder liquidity, which is unusual and disarming; business risks get five slides, named specifically; the funds slide is split by function with the headcount plan; the deck is openly in the founder's voice (comic-sans style captions, a mascot), which worked for a community-led brand and would not transfer to most companies. Fishkin wrote afterwards that the deck was over-long and the round took longer than planned.

## Pattern 8: famous failures and cautionary decks

### WeWork, Series D era investor memorandum, 2014, $335M (aggregators), 66 pages (partly viewed; structure from secondary description)
Source: https://www.alexanderjarvis.com/wework-pitch-deck-series-d/
What is visible: a mix of dense memorandum pages and slide pages titled Company Overview, Space as a Service, WeLive, Membership growth, Growing rapidly, Making city-level decisions, Five-year forecast, Leadership and vision, with repeated "welive" dividers. The document projects the five-year plan in detail and presents the business as a technology platform.
Lessons: a 66-page fundraising document is a memorandum, not a deck; the forecast tables were later the basis for the valuation that collapsed in 2019, so the lesson is that forecast density is not evidence; compare with Carta (Pattern 6), which covers a later-stage round in 23 slides.

### Theranos (see Pattern 5) is the second cautionary deck: revenue claims ahead of evidence, insiders taking most of the round, the technology proof in the last slides.

### Front, Series B, 2018, $66M, 4 of the slides are shown on the source page (structure from secondary description beyond those)

## What the famous decks have in common (only patterns visible in the decks viewed above)

1. The title of a slide is the claim. In Front, LinkedIn, Brex, Carta, Alan, Mixpanel and Divvy every slide title is a sentence that states what the slide proves; the body is evidence. Decks that use noun labels only (Uber 2008, YouTube 2005, Theranos) read as documents rather than arguments.
2. Length is bimodal. Seed decks that raised on traction are 7 to 14 slides (Intercom 7, TransferWise 9, Tinder 10, YouTube 10, Coinbase 11, Buffer 13, Airbnb 14). Series A and later decks are 19 to 42 slides, and the good ones use divider slides or an appendix to keep the main story short (Front 21 with a divider, Mattermark 15 plus 18 appendix, Alan 42 in six sections).
3. One number per slide. Airbnb's business model, Coinbase's $65,000 in five weeks, Front's 5.4x MRR, Monzo's 324 transactions a year, Carta's $50M ARR: the memorable slides each carry one headline figure with its unit and time frame. Dense metric slides exist (Moz, Mattermark) but sit after the headline ones.
4. Traction comes early when it exists. Buffer slide 5, TransferWise slide 4, Brex slide 15 of 19 but preceded by a problem shown as evidence, Front slide 8. Pre-traction decks replace it with "progress" (Intercom), "metrics" as a sentence (YouTube) or a validation proxy (Airbnb's Couchsurfing and Craigslist numbers).
5. The market is argued, not quoted. Airbnb and Brex use concentric or multiplied markets that end at the wedge; Dropbox, YouTube and Uber argue "why now" from technology shifts; LinkedIn and Carta build the market from comparables. No viewed deck leads with a single top-down TAM figure except Theranos.
6. Competition is drawn to show the gap. Airbnb's 2x2, Front's 2x2, BuzzFeed's Venn, Intercom's category table, Dropbox's "what did they screw up", Mixpanel's free vs paid grid. Each diagram's axes are chosen so the company occupies an empty space.
7. The ask is tied to an outcome. Airbnb ($500K to 80K trips and $2M revenue), TransferWise (GBP 650K to last until end of 2012 with four goals), Front ($10M then team, roadmap, projections), Mattermark (hiring plan at four horizons with ARR targets), Moz (use of funds by function). Decks without an explicit ask (Coinbase, Tinder, Dropbox, YouTube) are the ones with the steepest early growth or the strongest founder pedigree.
8. Regulated businesses show the licence or the precedent as proof. Monzo's licence timeline, Alan's "first independent insurer licensed in France since 1986", Brex's investor roster, LinkedIn's PayPal precedent for delaying revenue.
9. Analogies are used sparingly and precisely. Coinbase ("iTunes : MP3"), Uber ("NetJets of car services"), LinkedIn (eBay, PayPal, Google as "2.0"), Carta ("utility company, financial cables"), BuzzFeed ("YouTube/Wikipedia for buzz"). One analogy per deck, placed where the business model is explained.
10. Honesty slides age well. Moz's five named risks, Mattermark's "lessons learned from 6 months of SaaS sales", Carta labelling its vision examples as not current customers, Front's capital-efficiency numbers. The two cautionary decks (Theranos, WeWork) are the ones with the least verifiable content per page.
